How do you write down a measurement's outcome, its probability and the state it leaves behind as one object?

Take two qubits A and B in

\(\begin{aligned}|\psi\rangle = {}&\sqrt{\tfrac13}\,|0\rangle_A|0\rangle_B \\ &+ \sqrt{\tfrac23}\,|1\rangle_A|{+}\rangle_B\end{aligned}\)

and measure A in the Z basis. Outcome 0 comes with probability 1/3 and leaves B in \(|0\rangle\); outcome 1 comes with probability 2/3 and leaves B in \(|{+}\rangle\). Write the outcome into a classical record R, and keep each probability inside its branch instead of dividing it out:

\(\begin{aligned}\omega_{RB} = {}&|0\rangle\langle 0|_R \otimes \tfrac13 |0\rangle\langle 0| \\ &+ |1\rangle\langle 1|_R \otimes \tfrac23 |{+}\rangle\langle{+}|\end{aligned}\)

As a matrix, record first, it is block diagonal: one block per outcome.

\(\omega_{RB} = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac13 & 0 & & \\ 0 & 0 & & \\ & & \tfrac13 & \tfrac13 \\ & & \tfrac13 & \tfrac13 \end{pmatrix}\)

The recipe for any state and any projective measurement \(\{P_k\}\) on A:

\(\begin{gathered}\omega = \sum_k |k\rangle\langle k|_R \otimes \rho_k \\ \rho_k = \mathrm{Tr}_A\!\left[(P_k\otimes 1)\,\rho_{AB}\right] \\ p_k = \mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_k\end{gathered}\)

The blocks \(\rho_k\) are unnormalized on purpose: the trace of each block is the probability of that branch. So \(\omega\) is an ordinary density matrix (positive, trace 1) that holds every branch and its odds at once. It is called a classical–quantum state, or cq state (Wilde, arXiv:1106.1445). Every protocol in this course is a sequence of maps that take one cq state to another.

CheckedThe two blocks are \(\tfrac13|0\rangle\langle0|\) and \(\tfrac23|{+}\rangle\langle{+}|\); \(\omega\) has trace 1 and no negative eigenvalue. instruments_checks.py, L1–2.

Measure A of \(|{+}\rangle_A|0\rangle_B\) in Z. What is \(\omega_{RB}\)?

\(\tfrac12|0\rangle\langle0|\otimes|0\rangle\langle0| + \tfrac12|1\rangle\langle1|\otimes|0\rangle\langle0| = \tfrac{1}{2}1_R \otimes |0\rangle\langle 0|\). Both blocks hold the same state, so the record is uncorrelated with B: a product state.

What can you get out of \(\omega\) once you have it?

Three operations, each shown on the example of the last lesson, \(\omega = |0\rangle\langle0|\otimes\tfrac13|0\rangle\langle0| + |1\rangle\langle1|\otimes\tfrac23|{+}\rangle\langle{+}|\):

  • Probability of an outcome: the trace of its block. \(p_1 = \mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_1 = 2/3\).
  • Forget the outcome: trace out the record, which adds up the blocks.
    \(\mathrm{Tr}_R\,\omega = \rho_0 + \rho_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 2/3 & 1/3 \\ 1/3 & 1/3\end{pmatrix}\)
  • Postselect: keep one block and renormalize. Keeping outcome 1 gives \(\rho_1/p_1 = |{+}\rangle\langle{+}|\), and works in 2/3 of the runs.

The matrix you get by forgetting is exactly \(\mathrm{Tr}_A|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\), the state of B if A had never been measured. It must be: if measuring A changed B's state, whoever holds A could signal to B by choosing whether to measure.

Forgetting is a partial trace, a channel like any other. Postselection is the one operation that is not: it throws away the other blocks, so it is not trace preserving, and you pay for it by repeating the experiment \(1/p_k\) times on average.

Checked\(\mathrm{Tr}_R\,\omega = \mathrm{Tr}_A|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\) and \(\rho_1/p_1 = |{+}\rangle\langle{+}|\). instruments_checks.py, L1–2.

Which of the three operations would you use to compute the average of \(\langle Z_B\rangle\) over all runs, and what is it here?

Forget the outcome: \(\mathrm{Tr}(Z\,\mathrm{Tr}_R\,\omega) = 2/3 - 1/3 = 1/3\). Averages of observables only need the summed blocks.

Is a list of branches really more than its average?

Two sources each emit one qubit per run. Source Z sends \(|0\rangle\) or \(|1\rangle\) with probability 1/2 each; source X sends \(|{+}\rangle\) or \(|{-}\rangle\). Both average to \(1/2\): no measurement on the qubit alone can tell which source you have. Every mixed state has infinitely many such decompositions into ensembles, and they are all related by a unitary mixing the vectors \(\sqrt{p_k}|\psi_k\rangle\); here that unitary is the Hadamard (Hughston, Jozsa and Wootters, Phys. Lett. A 183, 14 (1993)).

Now let each source also write down which state it sent:

\(\begin{aligned}\omega_Z &= \tfrac12|0\rangle\langle0|\otimes|0\rangle\langle0| \\ &\;+ \tfrac12|1\rangle\langle1|\otimes|1\rangle\langle1| \\ \omega_X &= \tfrac12|0\rangle\langle0|\otimes|{+}\rangle\langle{+}| \\ &\;+ \tfrac12|1\rangle\langle1|\otimes|{-}\rangle\langle{-}|\end{aligned}\)

These are different states. Their trace distance is \(\tfrac12\|\omega_Z-\omega_X\|_1 = 1/\sqrt2\), so someone holding record and qubit can guess the source correctly 85% of the time, \(\tfrac12(1+1/\sqrt2)\) (Helstrom's bound, see Wilde arXiv:1106.1445). Without the record they can only do 50%.

So the branch picture is physical exactly when the branch label is stored somewhere. The cq state makes that storage explicit: the record is a system in the density matrix, not a story told beside it.

CheckedBoth cq states reduce to \(1/2\); their trace distance is 0.7071; the Hadamard maps one ensemble's vectors to the other's. instruments_checks.py, L3.

Can a pure state \(|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|\) be written as a mixture of different pure states?

No. A pure state has rank 1, so every vector in a decomposition must be proportional to \(|\phi\rangle\). Only mixed states have many ensembles.

Why can a record be read, copied and passed around freely, when a qubit cannot?

The record R is an ordinary quantum system whose state is always diagonal in one fixed basis. Three facts follow, each checked on the example \(\omega\) of the first lesson:

  • Reading it disturbs nothing. Measuring R in its own basis maps \(\omega \mapsto \sum_k (|k\rangle\langle k|\otimes 1)\,\omega\,(|k\rangle\langle k|\otimes 1) = \omega\).
  • Copying works. A CNOT from R onto a fresh bit in \(|0\rangle\) gives \(\sum_k |kk\rangle\langle kk|\otimes\rho_k\): two people now hold the same outcome. The same CNOT on a qubit in \(|{+}\rangle\) makes a Bell pair instead, whose halves are each maximally mixed.
  • Any qubit becomes a record by dephasing, \(\Delta(\rho) = \sum_k |k\rangle\langle k|\rho|k\rangle\langle k|\): measuring and not looking.

So "classical" just means "dephased in a known basis". No second formalism is needed: bits and qubits live in one density matrix, and the no-cloning theorem never bites on a record, because a diagonal state is a mixture of orthogonal basis states, and those can be copied.

CheckedDephasing R leaves \(\omega\) unchanged; CNOT onto \(|0\rangle\) copies the record exactly; on \(|{+}\rangle\) it gives a first qubit of purity 0.5. instruments_checks.py, L4.

Apply a Hadamard to the record of \(\omega = \sum_k|k\rangle\langle k|\otimes\rho_k\). When is the result still a cq state in the 0/1 basis?

The off-diagonal block becomes \(\tfrac12(\rho_0-\rho_1)\). It vanishes only if \(\rho_0 = \rho_1\), that is, when the record was uncorrelated with the qubit anyway.

What turns a quantum state into a cq state?

A quantum instrument: one map \(\mathcal E_k\) per outcome \(k\), each completely positive, written with Kraus operators,

\(\begin{gathered}\mathcal E_k(\rho) = \sum_j K_{kj}\,\rho\,K_{kj}^\dagger \\ \sum_{k,j} K_{kj}^\dagger K_{kj} = 1\end{gathered}\)

The condition on the right makes the probabilities \(p_k = \mathrm{Tr}\,\mathcal E_k(\rho)\) add up to 1. The output is the cq state \(\sum_k |k\rangle\langle k|\otimes\mathcal E_k(\rho)\).

A projective measurement has one Kraus operator per outcome, \(K_k = P_k\). A weak Z measurement has, with \(c = \cos\varphi\) and \(s = \sin\varphi\),

\(K_0 = \begin{pmatrix}c & 0\\ 0 & s\end{pmatrix},\qquad K_1 = \begin{pmatrix}s & 0\\ 0 & c\end{pmatrix}\)

Then \(p_0 = \cos^2\!\varphi\,\rho_{00} + \sin^2\!\varphi\,\rho_{11}\). At \(\varphi = 0\) it is the projective measurement; at \(\varphi = \pi/4\) it learns nothing; at \(\varphi = \pi/8\) a \(|0\rangle\) answers 0 in 85% of runs. On \(|{+}\rangle\) both outcomes have probability 1/2, and outcome 0 leaves \(\cos\varphi|0\rangle + \sin\varphi|1\rangle\): the state is pulled part of the way towards \(|0\rangle\), not all the way.

Read as one map, \(\rho\mapsto\sum_k|k\rangle\langle k|\otimes\mathcal E_k(\rho)\) is a channel (completely positive and trace preserving) from a qubit to record plus qubit. The idea goes back to Davies and Lewis (Commun. Math. Phys. 17, 239 (1970)).

CheckedThe Kraus operators satisfy the completeness condition, the formula for \(p_0\) holds for random inputs, and the channel's Choi matrix is positive. instruments_checks.py, L5.

What does the weak measurement do to the state at \(\varphi = \pi/4\)?

\(K_0 = K_1 = 1/\sqrt2\), so each branch is \(\rho/2\): a fair coin flip that neither learns about the state nor disturbs it.

Do the outcome probabilities fix what a measurement does to the state?

No. Compare two instruments on a qubit:

\(\begin{array}{l|cc} & K_0 & K_1 \\ \hline \text{Z measurement} & |0\rangle\langle 0| & |1\rangle\langle 1| \\ \text{reset} & |0\rangle\langle 0| & |0\rangle\langle 1|\end{array}\)

Both give \(p_k = \langle k|\rho|k\rangle\) for every input. But Z measurement leaves \(|k\rangle\) in branch \(k\), while reset leaves \(|0\rangle\) in both. Forgetting the outcome, the first is dephasing and the second is the reset channel \(\rho\mapsto|0\rangle\langle0|\).

The rule: probabilities depend only on the effects \(E_k = \sum_j K_{kj}^\dagger K_{kj}\) (together they form a POVM). The post-measurement states depend on the Kraus operators themselves, and any \(K_k \to U_k K_k\) with unitary \(U_k\) gives the same odds and different states. Among all instruments with given effects, \(K_k = \sqrt{E_k}\) is the Lüders instrument (Lüders, translated in arXiv:quant-ph/0403007). The weak measurement of the last lesson is one.

This is why "measure" on hardware has to say what happens to the qubit afterwards: measure-and-keep and measure-and-reset are different instruments with identical outcome statistics.

CheckedSame \(p_k\) for random inputs; reset leaves \(|0\rangle\) in both branches; \(K_k = \sqrt{E_k}\) for the weak measurement. instruments_checks.py, L6.

You only see the outcomes of a device. Can you tell whether it resets?

Not from one measurement. You need a later operation on the same qubit, for example measuring it again: Z measurement repeats outcome 1, reset gives 0.

How do states, channels and measurements fit into one picture?

Each is a channel between systems that are quantum (Q), classical (R) or trivial (1, nothing). They differ only in which slots are which:

ObjectMapExample
state1 → Qprepare \(|{+}\rangle\)
distribution1 → Ra coin flip
ensemble source1 → RQ\(\omega_Z\) of lesson 3
channelQ → Qan instrument with one outcome
POVMQ → Ran instrument, qubit discarded
instrumentQ → RQweak measurement
controlled operationRQ → RQapply \(X\) if the record says 1
stochastic mapR → Ra noisy classical bit

Two moves come for free. Forgetting is a partial trace: the Z instrument followed by \(\mathrm{Tr}_R\) is dephasing, and followed by \(\mathrm{Tr}_Q\) it is the POVM \(\rho\mapsto\rho_{00}|0\rangle\langle0| + \rho_{11}|1\rangle\langle1|\). Postselection is the only step that is not a channel.

The payoff is one composition rule (plug outputs into inputs) and one consistency condition (complete positivity and trace preservation). Measurement, preparation, feedforward and forgetting are no longer separate postulates. Textbook treatment: Watrous, The Theory of Quantum Information (2018).

Checked\(\mathrm{Tr}_R\) of the Z instrument is dephasing; \(\mathrm{Tr}_Q\) gives \(\mathrm{diag}(\rho)\) on the record. instruments_checks.py, L7.

Which slot pattern is a classically controlled preparation, like a BB84 sender choosing a state from two bits?

R → Q: \(|ab\rangle\langle ab|\mapsto\rho_{ab}\). It is an encoder, the mirror image of a POVM.

How is an instrument actually built?

The way a mid-circuit measurement is done on a quantum computer: bring in an ancilla in \(|0\rangle\), apply a CNOT from the system to the ancilla, and read the ancilla in Z. The system's branches are

\(\begin{aligned}\mathcal E_k(\rho) &= \mathrm{Tr}_{a}\big[\Pi^a_k\,U(\rho\otimes|0\rangle\langle0|_a)U^\dagger\big] \\ &= |k\rangle\langle k|\,\rho\,|k\rangle\langle k|\end{aligned}\)

with \(U\) the CNOT and \(\Pi^a_k = |k\rangle\langle k|\) on the ancilla. That is the Z instrument. The Kraus operators are read off the unitary as \(K_k = \langle k|_{\text{anc}}\,U\,|0\rangle_{\text{anc}}\). Swap the CNOT for a gentler controlled rotation, \(R_y(2\varphi)\) on the ancilla if the system is 0 and \(R_y(\pi - 2\varphi)\) if it is 1, and you get the weak measurement of lesson 5.

Every instrument arises this way: a probe in a fixed state, a joint unitary, and a projective readout of the probe,

\(\mathcal E_k(\rho) = \mathrm{Tr}_E\!\left[(1\otimes P_k)\,U(\rho\otimes\sigma)U^\dagger\right]\)

(Ozawa, J. Math. Phys. 25, 79 (1984)). It is the measurement version of writing a channel as a unitary on a larger system. A "measurement" is an interaction followed by reading a pointer, and the instrument is the bookkeeping of both. Mid-circuit measurements on real devices are benchmarked as instruments in exactly this sense (Rudinger et al., arXiv:2103.03008).

CheckedThe CNOT circuit reproduces the Z instrument for random inputs; the controlled rotations give exactly \(K_0, K_1\) of the weak measurement. instruments_checks.py, L8.

Leave out the CNOT but still read the ancilla. Which instrument is that?

Outcome 0 every time, with \(\mathcal E_0 = \mathrm{id}\): the identity channel with a useless record. No interaction, no information.

How does an operation that depends on an earlier outcome act on \(\omega\)?

Branch by branch. A classically controlled unitary is

\(\mathcal C\big(|k\rangle\langle k|\otimes\tau\big) = |k\rangle\langle k|\otimes U_k\,\tau\,U_k^\dagger\)

Because the record is diagonal, \(\mathcal C\) never mixes blocks; it rewrites each one with its own gate.

Example: reset by feedback. Measure a qubit in Z, then apply \(X\) if the outcome was 1:

\(\begin{gathered}|0\rangle\langle0|\otimes\rho_{00}|0\rangle\langle0| \\ + \,|1\rangle\langle1|\otimes\rho_{11}|1\rangle\langle1| \\ \Big\downarrow\,\mathcal C \\ \begin{pmatrix}\rho_{00} & 0\\0 & \rho_{11}\end{pmatrix}_R\otimes|0\rangle\langle0|\end{gathered}\)

Every branch now holds the same state, so the cq state is a product: the record has become plain noise and can be thrown away. This is the signature of a protocol that works deterministically despite random outcomes: after the corrections, \(\omega = \sigma_R\otimes\rho_{\text{out}}\). Teleportation and MBQC end this way.

CheckedFor random inputs the output is \(\mathrm{diag}(\rho_{00},\rho_{11})\otimes|0\rangle\langle0|\). instruments_checks.py, L9.

Apply \(H\) instead of \(X\) when the outcome is 1. Does the record decouple?

No: you get \(|0\rangle\langle0|\otimes\rho_{00}|0\rangle\langle0| + |1\rangle\langle1|\otimes\rho_{11}|{+}\rangle\langle{+}|\). The branches differ, so the record still tells you which state you hold.

Can classical control always be replaced by quantum control?

Yes. Compare, on any two-qubit input:

  • measure qubit 1 in Z, then apply \(X\) to qubit 2 if the outcome is 1;
  • apply CNOT from qubit 1 to qubit 2, then measure qubit 1 in Z.

They give the same cq state. The reason is one line: the controlled map \(\sum_k|k\rangle\langle k|\otimes U_k\) is the controlled-\(U\) gate, and it commutes with a Z measurement of its control, because both are diagonal on the control.

This is the principle of deferred measurement (Nielsen and Chuang, Quantum Computation and Quantum Information, §4.4). It lets you push every measurement to the end and analyse a feedforward protocol as a unitary circuit. The price is that the control qubit has to stay coherent until the end. Hardware with fast feedforward goes the other way: it measures early and reuses the qubit.

CheckedEqual cq states for random two-qubit mixed inputs. instruments_checks.py, L10.

Why does the swap fail if qubit 1 is measured in the X basis?

The controlled gate is diagonal in Z on the control, so it does not commute with an X measurement there. Classical control only exists in the basis the record is diagonal in.

How do you write a protocol whose next measurement depends on the last outcome?

Let the first instrument be \(\{\mathcal E_k\}\) and the second be chosen by \(k\), \(\{\mathcal F^{(k)}_l\}\). The record grows by one label per step:

\(\omega = \sum_{k,l} |k\,l\rangle\langle k\,l| \otimes \mathcal F^{(k)}_l\big(\mathcal E_k(\rho)\big)\)

Example: input \(\cos\tfrac\pi8|0\rangle + \sin\tfrac\pi8|1\rangle\). Measure Z; on outcome 0 measure X, on outcome 1 measure Z again.

The leaves are the blocks of \(\omega\), and the probability of a leaf is the trace of its block. Branch \((1,0)\) has probability 0: a second Z measurement repeats the first.

A whole adaptive protocol is still one channel, from the input to (record, output). In each run only one path is realized, but \(\omega\) holds all of them. The general theory of such networks, with quantum and classical wires between steps, is the theory of quantum combs (Chiribella, D'Ariano and Perinotti, arXiv:0904.4483).

Checked\(p(00) = p(01) = 0.4268\), \(p(10) = 0\), \(p(11) = 0.1464\). instruments_checks.py, L11.

Merge the leaves \((1,0)\) and \((1,1)\) into one label. Is the result still an instrument?

Yes. A sum of completely positive maps is completely positive, and the total is still trace preserving. Merging labels is how you forget part of a record.

What is the state of everything after each step of teleportation?

Registers: \(A'\) holds the input \(\rho\); \(A\) and \(B\) share \(|\Phi^+\rangle = (|00\rangle+|11\rangle)/\sqrt2\); \(R\) is a 2-bit record \(ab\). Label the Bell basis by \(|\beta_{ab}\rangle = (1\otimes X^aZ^b)|\Phi^+\rangle\). One identity does all the work:

\(\begin{aligned}(\langle\beta_{ab}|_{A'A}\otimes 1_B)&\,|\psi\rangle_{A'}|\Phi^+\rangle_{AB} \\ &= \tfrac12\,X^aZ^b|\psi\rangle_B\end{aligned}\)

Now the maps, one per step:

  • Prepare. \(\omega_0 = \rho_{A'}\otimes|\Phi^+\rangle\langle\Phi^+|_{AB}\).
  • Bell-measure \(A'A\), discard them. By the identity,
    \(\begin{aligned}\omega_1 = \tfrac14\sum_{ab}&|ab\rangle\langle ab|_R \\ &\otimes X^aZ^b\,\rho\,Z^bX^a\end{aligned}\)
  • Send \(R\) to Bob. The identity map on \(R\): only who holds it changes.
  • Correct. Apply \(Z^bX^a\) controlled by \(R\): \(\omega_2 = \tfrac14 1_R\otimes\rho\). The record decouples (lesson 9), so throw it away: Bob holds \(\rho\).

The composition of the four maps is the identity channel from \(A'\) to \(B\), for every input, with no postselection (Bennett et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 70, 1895 (1993)).

CheckedThe identity for random \(|\psi\rangle\); each block of \(\omega_1\) equals \(\tfrac14X^aZ^b\rho Z^bX^a\); \(\omega_2 = \tfrac14 1\otimes\rho\). instruments_checks.py, L12–13.

What is the probability of outcome \(ab = 11\)?

1/4, the trace of its block, whatever \(\rho\) is. The outcomes reveal nothing about the input.

Between Alice's measurement and Bob's correction, where is the input?

Read \(\omega_1 = \tfrac14\sum_{ab}|ab\rangle\langle ab|\otimes X^aZ^b\rho Z^bX^a\) with the moves of lesson 2:

  • Bob alone, \(\mathrm{Tr}_R\,\omega_1 = 1/2\): maximally mixed, independent of \(\rho\). That is why teleportation cannot send a signal faster than the record travels.
  • The record alone, \(\mathrm{Tr}_B\,\omega_1 = 1_R/4\): uniform noise.

All of \(\rho\) sits in the correlation between \(R\) and \(B\). Now break the classical link and see what reaches Bob:

  • Bob receives only \(a\), and applies \(X^a\): he gets \(\tfrac12(\rho + Z\rho Z)\), the input dephased.
  • Bit \(a\) is flipped in transit with probability \(q\): he gets \((1-q)\rho + q\,X\rho X\).

A noisy classical channel turns into a Pauli channel on the qubit. In the cq picture this is just a stochastic map R → R inserted before the controlled correction.

CheckedBoth marginals; the dephased output with only \(a\); the bit-flip output for \(q = 0.1\). instruments_checks.py, L12–13.

Bit \(b\) is flipped with probability \(q\) instead. What does Bob get?

\((1-q)\rho + q\,Z\rho Z\). The wrong correction leaves an extra \(Z\) on the branch.

What does an error-correction cycle look like as a cq state?

The bit-flip code stores a qubit as \(\alpha|000\rangle+\beta|111\rangle\). Let each qubit flip independently with probability \(p\). To find out which one flipped, measure the parities \(Z_1Z_2\) and \(Z_2Z_3\): two ancillas, four CNOTs, read the ancillas, exactly the construction of lesson 8. The result is a cq state with a 2-bit syndrome record \(s\):

\(\begin{gathered}\omega = \sum_s |s\rangle\langle s|\otimes\rho_s \\ \rho_{00} = (1-p)^3\,\rho_L + p^3\,X_L\rho_LX_L\end{gathered}\)

where \(\rho_L\) is the encoded state and \(X_L = X_1X_2X_3\). Each block mixes the two error patterns that give the same syndrome; for \(s = 10\) they are "qubit 1 flipped" and "qubits 2 and 3 flipped". At \(p = 0.1\):

\(\begin{aligned}p(00) &= (1-p)^3 + p^3 = 0.73 \\ p(s \ne 00) &= p(1-p)^2 + p^2(1-p) \\ &= 0.09\end{aligned}\)

The parity measurements never touch \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\): both parities are \(+1\) on \(|000\rangle\) and on \(|111\rangle\), so the record learns which error happened and nothing about the encoded state. A fault-tolerant computer repeats this every cycle as mid-circuit measurements (Gottesman, arXiv:0904.2557).

CheckedThe four syndrome probabilities; the ancilla circuit gives the same blocks as the parity projectors. instruments_checks.py, L14–15.

Why not measure \(Z_1\) directly to find a flip?

\(Z_1\) tells \(|000\rangle\) from \(|111\rangle\), so it would collapse \(\alpha|000\rangle+\beta|111\rangle\). Parities are blind to the encoded state.

How does a syndrome record become a better qubit?

Decoding is a classically controlled map: flip the qubit the syndrome points to, \(10\to X_1\), \(11\to X_2\), \(01\to X_3\), nothing on \(00\). Each block then holds the right state unless two or three qubits had flipped. Forgetting the syndrome,

\(\begin{gathered}\rho_{\text{out}} = (1-p_L)\,\rho_L + p_L\,X_L\rho_LX_L \\ p_L = 3p^2 - 2p^3\end{gathered}\)

At \(p = 0.1\), \(p_L = 0.028\). Error correction is feedforward, and what is left is a logical channel with a smaller error rate.

Unlike teleportation, the record does not fully decouple: how likely a logical error is depends on the syndrome. Keep only runs with syndrome 00 and the logical error drops to \(p^3/[(1-p)^3+p^3] = 0.0014\), at the cost of discarding 27% of runs. That is error detection: postselection on the syndrome record.

Since every correction here is a Pauli, a real device often does not apply it at all. It keeps the correction in software and reinterprets later outcomes, the same bookkeeping as the Pauli frame in MBQC.

Checked\(\rho_{\text{out}}\) with \(p_L = 0.0280\); postselected logical error 0.00137, kept in 73% of runs. instruments_checks.py, L14–15.

For which \(p\) does the code help, \(p_L < p\)?

\(3p^2 - 2p^3 < p \iff (2p-1)(p-1) > 0 \iff p < 1/2\).

What does measuring one qubit of a cluster do, branch by branch?

Use the conventions of the MBQC pocket course: measurement basis \(|s_\theta\rangle = (|0\rangle + (-1)^se^{i\theta}|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\) with outcome \(s\), and \(J(\theta) = H\,P(-\theta)\). Qubit 1 holds \(\rho\), qubit 2 starts in \(|{+}\rangle\). Three maps:

  • CZ on qubits 1 and 2;
  • the instrument \(\{|s_\theta\rangle\langle s_\theta|\}\) on qubit 1, outcome into a 1-bit record \(S\);
  • discard qubit 1.

With \(J = J(\theta)\), the result is

\(\omega = \tfrac12\sum_s|s\rangle\langle s|_S\otimes X^sJ\rho J^\dagger X^s\)

It has the same shape as teleportation's \(\omega_1\), with one record bit instead of two and a gate \(J(\theta)\) left behind. The "Bell pair" is a single CZ bond to a \(|{+}\rangle\) qubit.

Forgetting \(s\) keeps only the X component of the Bloch vector of \(J\rho J^\dagger\): without the record, the step is useless. MBQC does not undo the byproduct on the spot; it keeps the record (Raussendorf, Browne and Briegel, arXiv:quant-ph/0301052).

CheckedBoth blocks for random inputs at \(\theta = 0.9\); the forgotten state's Bloch vector is \((n_x, 0, 0)\). instruments_checks.py, L16.

Why is each outcome exactly 50% likely, whatever \(\rho\) is?

Each block is \(\tfrac12\) times a unitary applied to \(\rho\), so its trace is \(\tfrac12\). The outcome carries no information about the logical state, which is what lets the computation go on undisturbed.

How does the record decide the next measurement?

Chain two steps on a wire 1–2–3. After the first, qubit 2 holds \(X^{s_1}J(\theta_1)\rho\,J(\theta_1)^\dagger X^{s_1}\). The second measurement is an instrument controlled by \(S_1\): its angle is \((-1)^{s_1}\theta_2\). Using \(J(\varphi)X \simeq Z\,J(-\varphi)\) (equal up to a global phase), every branch comes out as

\(\begin{gathered}X^{s_2}Z^{s_1}\,U\rho\, U^\dagger\,Z^{s_1}X^{s_2} \\ U = J(\theta_2)J(\theta_1)\end{gathered}\)

so the right gates were applied in all four branches, up to a Pauli fixed by the record. Without adapting, branch \(s_1 = 1\) gets \(J(-\theta_2)\) instead of \(J(\theta_2)\), and no Pauli correction repairs that.

In the cq picture a controlled instrument is a map from (record, qubit) to (longer record, qubit), and it is no harder to write than a controlled gate. Which Pauli each branch carries is the Pauli frame of the MBQC course.

CheckedAll four adaptive branches at \(\theta_1 = 0.9\), \(\theta_2 = 0.4\); without adapting, the best Pauli-corrected fidelity in branch \(s_1 = 1\) is 0.907. instruments_checks.py, L17.

At \(\theta_2 = \pi/2\), does the second angle need adapting?

No. \(J(-\pi/2) = J(\pi/2)\,Z\) up to phase, so the "wrong" branch differs only by a Pauli, which the record absorbs. Clifford angles never wait for earlier outcomes.

Why can all the CZs of a computation be done before any measurement?

Write the two-step wire as a composition of maps, read right to left (\(\mathcal P_k\) prepares \(|{+}\rangle_k\), \(\mathcal Z_{jk}\) is a CZ, \(\mathcal M_k\) the measurement of qubit \(k\)):

\(\begin{aligned}\Phi = {}&\mathcal M_2\circ\mathcal Z_{23}\circ\mathcal P_3 \\ &\circ\mathcal M_1\circ\mathcal Z_{12}\circ\mathcal P_2\circ\mathcal P_1\end{aligned}\)

One rule moves things: maps on disjoint systems commute. \(\mathcal Z_{23}\circ\mathcal P_3\) touches qubits 2 and 3; \(\mathcal M_1\) touches qubit 1 and writes \(S_1\). So they swap, and

\(\begin{gathered}\Phi = \mathcal M_2\circ\mathcal M_1\big(|C_3\rangle\langle C_3|\big) \\ |C_3\rangle = \mathrm{CZ}_{23}\mathrm{CZ}_{12}|{+}{+}{+}\rangle\end{gathered}\)

Two things cannot move. \(\mathcal Z_{23}\) cannot pass \(\mathcal M_2\): both act on qubit 2. And \(\mathcal M_2\) cannot pass \(\mathcal M_1\): it reads \(S_1\). The classical control is the only time order left.

That is the resource-state picture of MBQC: the cluster is the same for every program, and the program lives in the angles and their feedforward (Raussendorf and Briegel, arXiv:quant-ph/0010033). It is also why photonic machines can weave the cluster on the fly, just ahead of the measurements.

CheckedThe sequential and cluster-first orders give the same cq state, branch by branch: each of the four blocks is \(\tfrac14\) times the projector onto \(X^{s_2}Z^{s_1}J(\theta_2)J(\theta_1)|{+}\rangle\). instruments_checks.py, L18.

Could \(\mathcal Z_{12}\) be applied after \(\mathcal M_1\)?

No. \(\mathcal M_1\) consumes qubit 1, so every edge at a qubit must come before that qubit is measured.

When does the cq state hold physics that the averaged state cannot show?

Take the GHZ state \((|000\rangle+|111\rangle)/\sqrt2\) on A, B, C and measure A, either in X or in Z.

  • X: each branch leaves BC in \((|00\rangle\pm|11\rangle)/\sqrt2\), a Bell pair: 1 ebit per branch.
  • Z: each branch leaves \(|00\rangle\) or \(|11\rangle\): no entanglement.

Yet forgetting the outcome gives the same \(\rho_{BC} = \tfrac12(|00\rangle\langle00| + |11\rangle\langle11|)\) in both cases, a separable state. The difference sits in a nonlinear function of the blocks, the average branch entanglement \(\sum_k p_k\,E(\rho_k/p_k)\), which \(\mathrm{Tr}_R\,\omega\) cannot see.

This is the entanglement a measurement can concentrate, called localizable entanglement (Verstraete, Popp and Cirac, arXiv:quant-ph/0307009). It is also the mechanism of measurement-induced phase transitions in monitored circuits: averaged over outcomes the state is featureless, and the transition appears only in branch-wise entropies (Skinner, Ruhman and Nahum, arXiv:1808.05953; Li, Chen and Fisher, arXiv:1808.06134). To see it in an experiment you must know which branch you are in, which is why these experiments face a postselection problem.

CheckedBranch entropies 1 and 1 ebit (X) or 0 and 0 (Z); the same averaged \(\rho_{BC}\), whose partial transpose has no negative eigenvalue. instruments_checks.py, L19.

Which measurement on A gives BC the most entanglement on average?

Any basis on the equator of the Bloch sphere, such as X or Y: each branch is a maximally entangled state of BC, so the average is 1 ebit.

You now have one object, the cq state, and one kind of move, a channel between quantum and classical systems. Where it leads:

  • Quantum combs and process tensors. A whole multi-step protocol with an unknown middle as one object (Chiribella, D'Ariano and Perinotti arXiv:0904.4483; Pollock et al. arXiv:1512.00589).
  • LOCC. What distant parties can do with local instruments and classical messages, and why it is hard to characterize (Chitambar et al. arXiv:1210.4583).
  • Information versus disturbance. An instrument that never disturbs any state learns nothing (Busch arXiv:0706.3526).
  • Instruments on real hardware. How errors of mid-circuit measurements are modelled and read off, one superoperator per outcome (Hashim arXiv:2604.18884).
  • Quantum trajectories. A Lindblad equation is the branch average of a continuously measured system; each trajectory is one path of the tree (Daley arXiv:1405.6694).
  • Long-range entanglement from measurement. Measurement plus feedforward prepares topological order in constant depth (Tantivasadakarn, Thorngren, Vishwanath and Verresen arXiv:2112.01519).
  • What is quantum about MBQC. The record and feedforward are classical; the power comes from contextual correlations in the resource (Anders and Browne arXiv:0805.1002; Raussendorf arXiv:0907.5449).

Which protocols in this course needed postselection to work?

None. Teleportation, error correction and MBQC all correct every branch with feedforward; postselection appeared only as an option, in error detection.
